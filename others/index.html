Research

My work is organized around a central question: how can computational methods help us understand complex social and economic phenomena? I approach this through three interconnected research streams that combine methodological innovation with substantive applications.

In computational social science, I study how information flows shape political and economic outcomes. This includes analyzing media coverage patterns across countries, detecting political rhetoric using LLM-based classification, and examining how symbolic policies like street naming reflect institutional change. I build custom data infrastructure, often involving automated scraping, NER pipelines, and geospatial matching, to study these questions at scale.

My work in machine learning and signal processing focuses on developing architectures that respect the mathematical structure of the data they process. Recent work explores complex-valued neural networks for audio applications, where preserving phase information improves downstream tasks. I have also worked on computer vision systems for video quality assessment and 3D reconstruction using SLAM and neural radiance fields.

In applied econometrics and market design, I develop theoretical models and empirical strategies to study markets with information frictions. This includes game-theoretic models of signaling in real estate and data markets, causal analyses of charitable giving behavior, and Bayesian methods for borrowing historical information in clinical trials. I am particularly interested in how economic theory can guide the design of efficient institutions.

IEEE Conference on Artificial Intelligence (CAI) 2026

Phase-Aware Deep Learning with Complex-Valued CNNs for Audio Signal Applications

Naman Agrawal

Proposes complex-valued CNN architectures and training adaptations including complex batch normalization, initialization schemes, and various complex activations to exploit phase information typically discarded by real-valued networks. Evaluates across three stages: image baselines, MFCC audio classification, and phase-aware graph neural networks. Results demonstrate that complex architectures match real CNN baselines while offering improved handling of phase in audio pipelines, with analysis of stability-expressivity trade-offs across different complex activation functions.

Analysis of Student-LLM Interaction in a Software Engineering Project

Naman Agrawal, Ridwan Shariffdeen, Guanlin Wang, Sanka Rasnayaka, and Ganesh N. Iyer

Tracks 126 undergraduate students over a 13-week semester, analyzing conversational logs, 730 LLM-generated code snippets, and code complexity metrics including lines of code, cyclomatic complexity, control flow graph depth, and Halstead effort. Working Papers

Cultural Flows in the Digital Age: Evidence from YouTube's Global Music Charts

Naman Agrawal

Analyzes 1.26 million weekly entries from YouTube Music's Top-100 charts across 60 countries (2021-2025) to map global music circulation patterns. Graph-based clustering reveals three stable listening blocs: a cohesive Latin American community, a broad global mainstream cluster linking Europe and Asian markets, and a distinct East African group. Bilateral similarity regressions confirm shared language as the strongest predictor of musical affinity, with diffusion networks revealing asymmetric export patterns centered on the United States and Mexico as the pivotal conduit for Spanish-language content.

Seventy-Five Years of the Supreme Court of India: An Empirical Analysis of Judicial Decision-Making, Citation Networks and Institutional Evolution

Naman Agrawal

Constructs a novel dataset of 42,833 judgments with structured metadata extracted using large language models, covering party characteristics, legal doctrines, constitutional provisions, precedent citations, bench composition, and case outcomes. Estimates determinants of appellant success using OLS, logit, probit, and high-dimensional fixed effects with causal identification through IV, RDD, and DiD designs. Maps citation networks to identify the 50 most influential precedents and detect doctrinal communities through Louvain clustering.

Crisis, Compassion, and Capital: The Economics of Global Charitable Giving

Naman Agrawal

Examines nearly 50,000 projects across 201 countries representing over $575 million in charitable donations (2002-2025). Uses the Russian invasion of Ukraine as a natural experiment through difference-in-differences and event study designs, finding 300%+ funding increases for Ukraine-related projects with evidence of both additionality and substitution effects. Text analysis reveals that urgency keywords increase funding by 35-40% and life-saving language by 89%, operating primarily through new donor acquisition rather than increased gift sizes.

Rigidity as Signal: A Game-Theoretic Explanation for Persistent Vacancies in Urban Real Estate Markets

Naman Agrawal

Models vacancy as a deliberate, costly signal in a two-period game with heterogeneous sellers, deriving multiple equilibrium types including pooling-holdout, pooling-discount, and both separating forms. Analyzes comparative statics and demonstrates how targeted policy levers such as vacancy taxes and price-ratio mandates can reshape equilibria toward more informative outcomes and improve allocative efficiency in urban housing markets.

Copula-Based Approaches to Engineering Simpson's Paradox and Applications to Fairness Evaluation

Naman Agrawal and Vishesh Gopal

Presents a reproducible copula-based pipeline using quantile inverse-CDF mapping, targeted copula dependence, assignment optimization, and simulated annealing to create tunable Simpson's paradox datasets for algorithmic fairness benchmarking. Demonstrates that pooled estimators can mask severe subgroup failures, making the method a practical testbed for fairness diagnostics.

Equilibrium Analysis for Strategic Information Revelation in Data Markets

Naman Agrawal

Develops a tractable signaling model where data sellers choose disclosure levels and buyers update beliefs accordingly. Derives conditions for pooling versus separating equilibria and uses simulations to demonstrate how disclosure costs and prior beliefs shape market vulnerability to adverse selection. Highlights policy implications including standardized disclosure requirements, certification mechanisms, and third-party audits.
